Executive summary

The policy is usually written. The enforcement system is not.

AI companies increasingly express policies in system prompts: what an agent may claim, what content a model may produce, what information an outbound message may contain, or when a tool should not be called. But a prompt is not a measurement or deployment layer. It cannot tell a team how much of a policy it catches, what it wrongly blocks, how behavior changes with context, or which cases deserve a more expensive model.

The product thesis is a policy experimentation and deployment platform. A customer imports a policy prompt or defines criteria, connects representative production traffic, refines examples and near-misses, compares models and context, and selects a calibrated threshold using recall, false-positive rate, traffic, latency, and cost. At runtime, a fast per-criterion scorer passes clean outputs, blocks or retries clear violations, and escalates uncertain or high-severity cases to a scoped LLM or remediation agent. Outcomes feed back into retraining, gated promotion, monitoring, and rollback.

This thesis comes from two independent production systems: consumer AI safety and enterprise email security. Both converged on the same architecture—fast scoring for the majority of traffic, selective reasoning for the residual, and a feedback loop that improves with customer data.

The durable product is not a universal policy classifier. It is the customer-specific control loop around the model.

The missing layer

Why policies in prompts are not enough

A support agent can make an unsupported claim while optimizing for resolution. An email agent can choose the wrong recipient after retrieved context changes. A chat model can produce disallowed content despite an explicit rule. These failures do not necessarily mean the policy was absent or badly written.

Policies compete with task completion, helpfulness, user instructions, and other objectives in the same context. Retrieved documents and tool outputs change what the model sees after the policy was authored. Long trajectories create more opportunities for drift and compounding errors. Prompt revisions change the behavior again, often without a stable evaluation set or operating point.

The practical problem is calibration. Teams need to map a policy change to violation recall, false-positive rate, affected traffic, escalation volume, latency, and cost. A binary LLM verdict does not provide that control. Nor does running a large judge on every request create the customer-specific data and deployment discipline needed for a system that improves.

Two production data points

The same missing layer appeared in two industries

Consumer AI safety

The original production detector was a similarity lookup over a curated bank of known bad messages. It was fast, but it could not learn a new pattern unless a close example was already present. I replaced it with a learned classifier over embeddings and built the surrounding operating system: LLM-assisted labeling, nightly retraining, threshold calibration, an automatic promotion gate, model versioning, monitoring, rollback, and sub-millisecond ONNX serving.

On a 50K-message side-by-side shadow sample, recall on explicit violations moved from 34% to 82%. Live recall held near 76% across three measured days using a broader label definition that included borderline cases. The selected threshold deliberately traded a higher false-positive rate for higher recall; calibration made that choice visible rather than accidental.

Enterprise email security

At an enterprise email-security company, I built a misdirected-email detection product from zero to one in about three months, including research, evaluation harnesses, backend, and inference integration. The architecture was a cascade: a lightweight ML gate, followed by a fine-tuned LLM, followed by a reasoning LLM API for the difficult residual.

Detection F1 improved from approximately 0.31 to approximately 0.75. I also fine-tuned and self-hosted Qwen3-4B using PEFT/QLoRA. That model replaced a hosted API tier serving more than 100K requests per day at roughly five times lower inference cost and lower latency.

Experiment program

What 450K messages changed

The initial product hypothesis was attractive: pretrain a universal router across many policy clauses, transfer it to a new customer from policy text alone, and win primarily on cost and latency. I tested that assumption across 450K production messages and roughly 1,900 policy clauses. The program compared managed and self-hosted embeddings, richer conversational context, larger backbones, token-level architectures, policy-conditioned models, and cross-policy pretraining.

Context and stronger backbones helped. Elaborate token-level architectures generally did not. Pretraining provided a useful cold start, but its transfer weakened when the new distribution was materially different from the training policies. In that out-of-domain experiment, a customer-specific model became stronger after roughly one hundred real labels.

Most comparisons used a time-based train/validation/test split. Token-architecture experiments were directional single-seed runs on a 100K subset due to a 132.7GB token cache; their ranking should not be treated as final-precision evidence.

What changed

The classifier is the receipt, not the product

Initial thesis

  • One universal policy scorer
  • Cost and latency as the main wedge
  • Architecture novelty as the advantage
  • Policy text as sufficient supervision

After the experiments

  • Customer-specific policy deployment platform
  • Calibration and feedback as the wedge
  • Data loop and promotion discipline as the moat
  • Pretraining as cold-start assistance

Cost and latency still matter, particularly at high request volume, but model providers will continue to become cheaper and faster. The defensible layer is learnability and control: a score the customer can threshold, production labels that train the next model, criteria that reflect the customer’s actual policy, and an auditable chain from model version to operating threshold to action.

Own the score, the labels, and the gradient pipe.

The product

A policy experimentation and deployment platform

A customer begins with an existing system prompt, policy document, or baseline criteria library. The platform applies that policy to a representative traffic sample and surfaces likely violations, near-misses, and clean examples. The customer refines those examples rather than writing a perfect specification upfront.

The experiment layer compares model families, features, context windows, thresholds, and escalation strategies. The calibration view translates model output into an operational decision: expected recall, false-positive rate, affected traffic, LLM volume, latency, and cost. The selected configuration compiles into a fast per-criterion scorer deployed through SaaS, a customer VPC, or an on-prem container.

At runtime, clean outputs pass without an expensive reasoning call. Clear violations block or retry. Uncertain or high-severity cases go to a tightly scoped LLM or remediation agent, which can return a revised output. Decisions and outcomes become evaluation and training data for the next cycle.

Initial wedge

Start where a wrong outbound action has a visible cost

The broad platform can support content safety, agent quality, outbound email, support routing, and regulated workflows. The sharper initial wedge is AI agents and outbound communication: messages, claims, tool calls, and state-changing actions where a policy failure has an identifiable owner and consequence.

This wedge needs to be earned through customer discovery. The central questions are whether the buyer already feels the pain, whether existing policies factor into useful criteria, how quickly customer labels improve over the baseline, how much reasoning traffic selective escalation avoids, and whether the preferred deployment is SaaS, VPC, or on-prem.

The strongest activation flow may be a baseline plus a refiner rather than a blank policy editor. A customer connects a traffic slice; the system shows what a baseline policy would flag; the customer marks examples as correct, incorrect, or ambiguous; and the platform previews how those decisions change the operating point. That produces value and customer-specific labels at the same time.

Longer-term direction

Use language as an interface, not the entire computation

The current product thesis is pragmatic: calibrated scoring, selective escalation, and a closed learning loop. A longer research direction asks whether agent evaluation and reasoning should remain bound to one growing prompt and token-by-token intermediate language.

RLM-style systems externalize context and inspect it programmatically. Action-native harnesses can evaluate structured state transitions rather than only textual outputs. JEPA-like and latent-reasoning approaches may move some useful computation into representation space, decoding language only when the interface requires it.

These are research directions, not current product claims. The near-term product does not depend on them. The common principle is narrower: use the cheapest representation and model that can make each decision reliably, and reserve expensive language reasoning for cases that genuinely need it.
