The problem

Rules in a prompt do not guarantee safe behavior

AI agents can violate policy even when the rules are explicit. A support agent may make an unsupported claim to close a ticket. A chat model may produce disallowed content while trying to remain helpful. An email agent may send sensitive information to the wrong person after context or a tool result changes.

Support“Resolve the issue” wins over “do not make unsupported commitments.”
ChatA long conversation drifts beyond a safety rule stated at the start.
EmailRetrieved context is correct, but the recipient or disclosure is not.

These failures happen because policies compete with task instructions, context and tool outputs change what the model sees, and longer agent trajectories create more chances to drift. Prompt changes are also difficult to evaluate against a stable operating point.

Why now

AI systems are moving from generating drafts to producing externally visible messages, calling tools, and changing state. Retrieval and multi-step execution make their context more dynamic, while falling inference costs increase the number of decisions they make. The policy layer has not kept pace: rules still live primarily as prose inside prompts, and most teams lack a standardized way to measure and enforce them at runtime.

The missing layer is a fast, calibrated way to detect these failures in production and decide what passes, retries, is rewritten, blocked, or escalated.

I want to build a policy experimentation and deployment platform. A company defines the policies its agents or outbound communication must follow; the platform learns from its traffic and gives every output a calibrated score for each criterion. Teams can measure violation rates, inspect recall, false-positive, traffic, latency, and cost trade-offs, and decide what passes, is blocked or retried, or is escalated to an LLM or remediation agent. Feedback becomes training data, so the system improves as policies and traffic change.

I arrived at this thesis by building the complete production loop for consumer AI safety, encountering the same routing problem independently in enterprise email security, and then using 450K messages to test whether the reusable product was a model or the system around it. The evidence changed the thesis: customer-specific calibration and feedback mattered more than a universal policy model.

Why this happens

Generation and enforcement are different jobs

A generative model is balancing many objectives at once: follow the policy, complete the task, use new context, respond helpfully, and act through tools. The policy is only one input to that optimization. As the context grows, later instructions, retrieved documents, and intermediate actions can change which objective dominates.

Enforcement requires a different set of capabilities. It needs stable evaluation data, a score rather than only a verdict, per-criterion thresholds, a known false-positive budget, and an action attached to each operating region. It also needs production outcomes to return as labels so the system adapts when traffic or policy changes.

This does not mean every request should be sent to an LLM judge. A large model is useful for ambiguous or severe cases, but using it everywhere is slower, more expensive, difficult to calibrate, and still does not create the customer-specific deployment loop.

Two production data points

The same missing layer appeared in two industries

Consumer AI safety

The original production detector was a similarity lookup over a curated bank of known bad messages. It was fast, but it could not learn a new pattern unless a close example was already present. I replaced it with a learned classifier over embeddings and built the surrounding operating system: LLM-assisted labeling, nightly retraining, threshold calibration, an automatic promotion gate, model versioning, monitoring, rollback, and sub-millisecond ONNX serving.

On a 50K-message side-by-side shadow sample, recall on explicit violations moved from 34% to 82%. Live recall held near 76% across three measured days using a broader label definition that included borderline cases. The selected threshold deliberately traded a higher false-positive rate for higher recall; calibration made that choice visible rather than accidental.

Enterprise email security

At an enterprise email-security company, I built a misdirected-email detection product from zero to one in about three months, including research, evaluation harnesses, backend, and inference integration. The architecture was a cascade: a lightweight ML gate, followed by a fine-tuned LLM, followed by a reasoning LLM API for the difficult residual.

Detection F1 improved from approximately 0.31 to approximately 0.75. I also fine-tuned and self-hosted Qwen3-4B using PEFT/QLoRA. That model replaced a hosted API tier serving more than 100K requests per day at roughly five times lower inference cost and lower latency.

What a company gets

A controllable outcome—not another model score

The buyer should be able to choose an operating point in business terms: catch x percent of violations, disturb y percent of clean traffic, route z percent to remediation, and understand the resulting latency and compute cost. The Kavana system made that trade-off explicit.

Quality at a known traffic budget

For a new customer, the primary deliverable is a calibrated curve: recall at 1%, 2%, 5%, and 10% false-positive budgets, plus the resulting block, retry, review, and LLM-escalation volumes. This converts “make the model safer” into an operating decision that finance, product, safety, and infrastructure teams can share.

Lower inference cost without losing control

On the measured 300M embedding service, compute cost ranged from approximately $9 per 10M short-message requests to $27 per 10M worst-case 1,000-token requests on an L4, excluding labeling, storage, orchestration, and platform overhead. Multi-turn inputs were approximately $13 per 10M requests. Self-hosting the same 300M model and GPU class reduced always-on instance cost by about 23%, or roughly $2,071 per year per instance, with no measured quality loss.

A stronger 4B backbone improved recall at 5% FPR from 49.8% to 59.1%, but estimated compute rose to roughly $250–370 per 10M requests on an L4. The platform benefit is therefore not “always use the smallest model.” It is the ability to quantify whether an extra 9.3 points of recall justify the additional compute, or whether context enrichment and selective escalation produce a better operating point.

Less operational burden

The loop retrained nightly on a rolling window, calibrated against clean traffic, evaluated challengers, promoted only when quality gates passed, published a versioned model, and retained one-command rollback. A company gets faster iteration without asking an engineer to manually rebuild the evaluation and deployment process for every policy change.

All cost figures above are measured infrastructure costs or explicitly labeled estimates from the Kavana experiments; they are not product pricing or a forecast of total customer cost.

Experiment program

What 450K messages changed

The initial product hypothesis was attractive: pretrain a universal router across many policy clauses, transfer it to a new customer from policy text alone, and win primarily on cost and latency. I tested that assumption across 450K production messages and roughly 1,900 policy clauses. The program compared managed and self-hosted embeddings, richer conversational context, larger backbones, token-level architectures, policy-conditioned models, and cross-policy pretraining.

Context and stronger backbones helped. Elaborate token-level architectures generally did not. Pretraining provided a useful cold start, but its transfer weakened when the new distribution was materially different from the training policies. In that out-of-domain experiment, a customer-specific model became stronger after roughly one hundred real labels.

Most comparisons used a time-based train/validation/test split. Token-architecture experiments were directional single-seed runs on a 100K subset due to a 132.7GB token cache; their ranking should not be treated as final-precision evidence.

Cost and latency still matter, particularly at high request volume, but model providers will continue to become cheaper and faster. The defensible layer is learnability and control: a score the customer can threshold, production labels that train the next model, criteria that reflect the customer’s actual policy, and an auditable chain from model version to operating threshold to action.

The product

A policy experimentation and deployment platform

A customer begins with an existing system prompt, policy document, or baseline criteria library. The platform applies that policy to a representative traffic sample and surfaces likely violations, near-misses, and clean examples. The customer refines those examples rather than writing a perfect specification upfront.

The experiment layer compares model families, features, context windows, thresholds, and escalation strategies. The calibration view translates model output into an operational decision: expected recall, false-positive rate, affected traffic, LLM volume, latency, and cost. The selected configuration compiles into a fast per-criterion scorer deployed through SaaS, a customer VPC, or an on-prem container.

Why this is not just an evaluation tool

Evaluation products help teams compare prompts and models before deployment. Guardrails and moderation APIs return a provider-defined verdict. Internal ML platforms can assemble parts of the loop, but each company must build policy ingestion, labeling, calibration, routing, monitoring, and promotion itself. This product connects evaluation to the runtime action and then learns from the outcome.

Why not use one LLM judge?

A scoped LLM remains part of the system for uncertain and severe cases. The fast scorer exists to provide a tunable probability, predictable traffic, low latency, and customer-specific learning. The product is the combination: calibrated scoring for control, reasoning where needed, and feedback that improves both.

Initial wedge + GTM

Start where a wrong outbound action has a visible cost

The broad platform can support content safety, agent quality, outbound email, support routing, and regulated workflows. The sharper initial wedge is AI agents and outbound communication: messages, claims, tool calls, and state-changing actions where a policy failure has an identifiable owner and consequence.

Buyer and entry point

The likely first buyer is the technical owner of agent reliability, trust and safety, or ML infrastructure at a company already operating meaningful AI traffic. The entry point is not a platform sale. It is a fixed-scope policy audit on the customer’s own data: connect 10K–50K historical events, import one existing policy prompt, and return a calibrated report showing misses, false positives, threshold choices, and projected escalation cost.

Land and expand

The audit becomes a shadow deployment for one high-value criterion set. The customer initially uses the system to measure rather than block. Once the operating point is trusted, the scorer begins routing uncertain cases and blocking only clear violations. Expansion comes from additional policies, agents, teams, and production actions.

The activation flow is a baseline plus a refiner rather than a blank policy editor. A customer connects a traffic slice; the system shows what a baseline would flag; the customer marks examples as correct, incorrect, or ambiguous; and the platform previews how those decisions change the operating point. That produces value and customer-specific labels at the same time.

Why this can be a company

Begin with one policy surface; expand into the control plane for agent actions

The wedge is outbound AI communication, where failures are externally visible and the trade-off between misses and false positives has a clear owner. The first deployment proves one criterion set on one traffic stream. Expansion comes from additional policies, agents, teams, and actions within the same customer.

The larger thesis is that consequential AI systems need a layer between generation and action. That layer decides whether an output can proceed, records why, routes exceptions, and improves from what happened next. If the pattern repeats across outbound messages, support decisions, tool calls, and other state-changing actions, the product becomes the policy control plane across the customer’s agent stack.

What compounds

Each customer accumulates structured criteria, reviewed boundary cases, calibrated operating points, and production outcomes. Those assets make the deployment more accurate and harder to replace. With permission, reusable criteria and cold-start models can improve activation within a vertical; the customer-specific data remains the source of production quality.

What is still unproven

The evidence establishes technical feasibility, not a market. The open questions are whether policy enforcement has a dedicated budget, whether the workflow is sufficiently consistent across customers, and whether teams prefer a platform over internal tooling. The GTM and next steps below are designed to answer those questions before broadening the product.

Next steps

Prove one repeatable workflow

The immediate objective is to show that an existing policy prompt and real production traffic can be turned into a trusted operating point—and that a buyer will deploy it.

Validate the wedge

Talk to teams running outbound agents or communication workflows. Collect real policy prompts, failure examples, review processes, and traffic economics. Confirm that policy failures have an owner, a measurable consequence, and budget.

Package the policy audit

Build a repeatable offline report over one customer data export. Show violations and near-misses, recall at several false-positive budgets, projected block and escalation traffic, latency, and compute cost. Use the audit to recruit design partners rather than asking them to buy a platform upfront.

Build the narrow technical loop

Ship policy import, criteria extraction, dataset sampling, assisted labeling, a strong pooled baseline, threshold sweeps, and a shadow scoring API. The minimum loop is label → train → calibrate → shadow → review. Start with one deployment path and one scoped LLM fallback.

Run production shadows

Measure drift, disagreement, latency, escalation volume, and cost without blocking traffic. Convert a shadow to enforcement only after the customer trusts the operating point. Add versioning, promotion gates, rollback, and automated retraining when the deployment requires them.

Success criteria

Three design partners willing to share traffic and a policy; a useful customer-specific operating point within roughly 100–200 labels; one repeatable integration pattern; and at least one paid pilot.

Keep the first build narrow

Defer a general policy DSL, multimodal enforcement, a large criteria marketplace, complex token-level attribution, multiple deployment modes, and fully automated promotion. Each expands the surface area before the core question—whether customers will buy calibrated control over a real workflow—is answered.
